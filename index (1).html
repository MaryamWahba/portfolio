About me Full history ↗

I teach the foundations, and I do the research.

I'm a teaching assistant in the AI department at Benha University and a Master's researcher. My students are young adults working through the foundational computer science and AI subjects they need to graduate and go on to work as AI engineers — so what I teach has to be correct, and it has to actually land, because their first job depends on it.

I don't teach from a curriculum somebody handed me. I research and write the material myself, from primary sources — papers, documentation and real datasets — and I write the assessments that go with it. That's the same work whether it's a semester at university or a module for a national training programme.

The syllabus isn't the whole job. Alongside the subject I give students an honest overview of what the real job market looks like — what employers actually ask for, what a first role involves, where the gap sits between a degree and being hireable. And when a student needs more than the lecture, I sit with them individually, on the technical side or the soft-skills side, depending on what's actually holding them back.

Alongside teaching, I publish. I'm first author on a peer-reviewed survey of 31 clinical AI studies, and a second paper is in progress. This isn't a separate hobby: AI content goes out of date in months, and reading the primary literature is what stops me teaching something that stopped being true last year.

I grew up and did my schooling in Abu Dhabi, UAE, and moved to Egypt for my final year of high school. That's where the bilingual part comes from — I teach technical material in Arabic or English without either being a translation of the other.

How I prepare a subject before I teach it

Not a framework — just the sequence I actually follow, whether it's a university course or a module for a national programme. It's the part of the job most people don't see, and the part that transfers to any teaching or training role.

01

Research it properly first

I go to the primary sources — papers, documentation, real datasets — before I write a single slide. I want to understand the method well enough to know what's genuinely settled and what's still contested, because a student will eventually ask.

02

Build the material and the assessment together

I write the explanation and the quiz at the same time, so the questions test whether the idea landed rather than whether someone memorised a definition. This is also the work I did on the DEPI programme.

03

Teach it, then fix what didn't land

I watch where a cohort loses the thread and redesign that explanation, instead of repeating the original one more slowly. The version that finally works usually looks nothing like the one I started with.

Four numbers I keep going back to

They came out of my own survey, but I don't only use them in a lecture. They're the reference points I reach for whenever I'm judging a claim about AI — reviewing someone's material, sitting in on a session, advising a student, or deciding what's worth building.

In the studies reviewed, GPT-4 produced inaccurate drug dosage recommendations in 14% of generated diagnostic summaries. A measured failure rate does work that a general warning never does — it changes the conversation from whether to trust a system to how often it's wrong and who checks. I ask for that number before I'll sign off on anything, my own material included.
Models trained on MIMIC-III performed 20% worse for rural populations than urban ones, because of who was in the training data. The habit it left me with is asking who's missing — from a dataset, from a room, from the group a programme was designed around. It's the same question whether the subject is a model or a curriculum.
Parameter-efficient methods reached roughly 95% of full fine-tuning performance while updating a fraction of the model — 18 GPU-hours against 120 in one comparison. The biggest option is rarely the right one, and the last few percent usually costs more than it returns. That applies to compute, to course length, and to how much scope a project really needs.
Clinicians trusted models they could question — trust scores rose from 2.4 to 4.1 out of 5 where explanations were provided. Yet fewer than 15% of the reviewed work used interpretability techniques at all. A field can measure the thing it finds easy to measure and miss the thing its users actually need. I check for that gap in any programme or product I'm asked to look at.

If you've read this far, here's the part that isn't on a CV.

What actually keeps me up is whether it landed. I can usually feel the moment a room stops following me, and it bothers me until I've pulled that explanation apart and built it again. Most of my best material is the third attempt at something that didn't work the first time, and I've stopped being embarrassed about that.

I'd rather be told I'm wrong early than be handled politely. It's why I go to the papers myself instead of trusting a summary, and why, if I don't know something yet, I'll say so out loud and then go and find out.

Both languages are properly mine. A lecture in Arabic isn't a thinner version of the English one — it gets written from scratch, with its own examples, because the people in front of me deserve the same lesson either way.

So: if you're building a course, a training track or a curriculum, and you want it to be accurate as well as teachable, that's the work I want next. Tell me who needs teaching and what they need to walk out able to do.
